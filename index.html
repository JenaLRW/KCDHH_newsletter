Inclusivity at the Dinner Table

The holiday season brings families and friends together for meals and celebrations. For people who are deaf or hard of hearing, these gatherings can also present communication barriers.

“Dinner table syndrome” is a term that describes being left out of conversation at family meals and other group gatherings. It can affect people with any degree of hearing loss, from mild to profound. Several people may talk at once. Background noise and dim lighting can make conversation hard to follow. A person may catch only part of what is said.

In this video from Kentucky DHH Behavioral Health Services, Anthony shares what dinner table syndrome has been like for him as a deaf person. You will also learn about the Dinner Table Project, which helps families find ways to break down communication barriers so no one is left out.

The tips below can help everyone take part.

For Family Members and Friends

  • Ask your family member or friend who is deaf or hard of hearing what helps most.
  • Offer a seat with a clear view of everyone at the table.
  • Keep the room well lit so faces are easy to see.
  • Turn off the television or music during the meal.
  • Take turns speaking so only one person talks at a time.
  • Get the person’s attention before you begin speaking.
  • Let the person know when the topic of conversation changes.
  • Repeat or rephrase information when asked. Avoid responding with “never mind” or “I’ll tell you later.”

For People Who Are Deaf or Hard of Hearing

  • Tell your host or family ahead of time what helps you follow conversation.
  • Choose a seat with a clear view of faces.
  • Sit away from sources of background noise when possible.
  • Check that hearing aids, cochlear implants, or other hearing devices are charged before the gathering.
  • Use a speech-to-text app on a phone or tablet to caption conversation at the table.
  • Ask a family member or friend to fill you in when you miss part of a conversation.
  • Spend time in smaller conversations, which can be easier to follow.
  • Take breaks when you need them. Following group conversation can be tiring.

Small changes can help everyone at the table share in the holiday conversation.

Diabetes Awareness Month

November is Diabetes Awareness Month. It is a good time to learn how diabetes can affect hearing health.

According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), diabetes can damage nerves in many parts of the body, including the ears. Over time, high blood sugar can damage small blood vessels and nerves in the inner ear. Low blood sugar over time can affect how nerve signals travel from the inner ear to the brain. The CDC reports that both types of damage can lead to hearing loss.

The CDC also reports that hearing loss is twice as common in people who have diabetes as in people of the same age who do not. People with prediabetes have a 30 percent higher rate of hearing loss than people with normal blood sugar levels.

Hearing loss can develop slowly. Some people may not notice the change right away. The CDC lists several signs of hearing loss, including often asking others to repeat themselves, having trouble following conversations with more than one person, and having trouble hearing in noisy places. Problems with the inner ear can also affect balance.

The CDC recommends several steps to help protect hearing. Keep blood sugar as close to target levels as possible. Have hearing tested by an audiologist after a diabetes diagnosis and every year after that. Avoid other causes of hearing loss, such as loud noise. Ask a doctor whether any current medications can affect hearing.

Hearing health is an important part of diabetes care for everyone, including people who are already deaf or hard of hearing.

KCDHH TAP Advisory Board Vacancies

Deadline to apply: December 2, 2026

The Kentucky Commission on the Deaf and Hard of Hearing (KCDHH) is accepting applications for three vacancies on the Telecommunications Access Program (TAP) Advisory Board:

  • Two deaf consumer representatives
  • One speech-impaired consumer representative

The TAP Advisory Board helps guide decisions about specialized telephone and telecommunications equipment for people in Kentucky who are deaf, hard of hearing, or have speech disabilities. Your experiences and perspectives can help ensure that TAP equipment and services reflect the needs of the people who use them.

Deaf consumer representatives must be deaf or hard of hearing. Speech-impaired consumer representatives must have a speech disability that affects their ability to communicate over the telephone.

Members are appointed to four-year terms. The board meets twice a year in Frankfort, typically in April and October.

The Kentucky School for the Deaf Advisory Board Will Meet Nov. 19

The Kentucky School for the Deaf (KSD) Advisory Board will meet in person at 6 p.m. ET / 5 p.m. CT on Nov. 19 in the Kerr Building Open Area at KSD in Danville. Watch live on the KSD YouTube Channel.

Per KRS 167.037, this board is to be composed of nine members appointed by the Kentucky Board of Education (KBE) upon the recommendation of the commissioner of education.

The purpose of the board is to act in an advisory capacity to assist school leadership and make recommendations related to: (1) school goals and objectives; (2) budget requests; (3) student services; (4) public relations; (5) construction and maintenance; and (6) program evaluation.

Membership on the board is statewide and consists of two parents of Deaf children, one professional in education of the Deaf, one former student of the Kentucky School for the Deaf, one member of the Kentucky Association for the Deaf (KAD), two members who represent school districts, and two members at large. A majority of the board’s membership is persons who are Deaf or Hard of Hearing.

Scam Alert

Scam Calls Are Getting More Convincing

The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) has warned that scammers are using new technology to make phone calls more convincing. For example, a scammer can use artificial intelligence to copy the voice of a loved one from a short audio clip posted online.

According to the FTC, consumers reported losing $15.9 billion to fraud in 2025. Reported losses have increased each year for six years in a row. FTC data also shows that scams starting with a phone call had the highest median loss per person, at $1,835.

Scam calls can reach any phone, including captioned telephones and phones used with relay services.

Protect yourself

  • Hang up if a caller pressures you to send money or share personal information.
  • Call back using a number you know is real, such as the number on your bank statement or the back of your card.
  • Talk with someone you trust before you send money.
  • Report scams to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.

Senior Planet from AARP offers free online classes on how to spot and avoid scams. Visit seniorplanet.org/classes to find an upcoming class.

Making Economics and Financial News Accessible in ASL!

Understanding the economy, national debt, and financial news can be complicated. Now, there’s a YouTube channel working to make these topics more accessible to the Deaf community!

Beyond the Headline, created by Michael Moore, features videos produced entirely in American Sign Language (ASL) with English captions. The videos break down complex topics, including the national debt, the Federal Reserve, financial markets, and major events that affect our everyday lives.

Document Submission Guidelines

KCDHH accepts submissions relevant to the Deaf and Hard of Hearing community, their families, service providers, and community partners. This includes events, announcements, and resources.

Submissions should include:

• Title of event or announcement

• Date and time

• Location (in-person or virtual)

• Contact information for person who can answer questions related to the information

• Brief description (100 words or fewer)

• Must state whether or not accommodations will be provided and if not, the name and contact information for the person that needs to be contacted to request it.

Information must be submitted in PDF format attached to an email.

All submitted documents must meet federal accessibility standards. KCDHH does not edit documents for accessibility. If your document is not accessible, please provide a direct link to a webpage or social media page where the information can be found and we will share the link.

KCDHH reserves the right to edit, accept, or decline any submission.
